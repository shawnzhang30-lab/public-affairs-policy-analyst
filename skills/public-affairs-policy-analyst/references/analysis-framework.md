# Analysis Framework

Use this reference when the task requires more than a factual summary.

## A. Institutional analysis

Ask five questions:

1. **Authority** — Which institution can formally decide?
2. **Instrument** — Law, regulation, guidance, enforcement, funding, procurement, licensing, informal coordination?
3. **Procedure** — What steps precede a binding decision?
4. **Implementation** — Who converts the rule into practice?
5. **Discretion** — Where can interpretation or enforcement vary?

This prevents the common error of treating every political statement as an operational requirement.

## B. Business-impact chain

Never jump directly from “policy change” to “business risk.”

Build the chain:

> Signal → authority → implementation → operational change → stakeholder response → business consequence

Example:

> Draft requirement → regulator obtains reporting authority → platform must create new reporting process → moderation/product teams change workflow → creators and users experience new friction → compliance cost and trust effects emerge.

The example is structural, not a claim about any real policy.

## C. Stakeholder map

Score only when useful; qualitative analysis is often sufficient.

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Authority | Can this actor formally decide or enforce? |
| Influence | Can the actor shape decision-makers or public interpretation? |
| Interest | How strongly is the actor affected? |
| Position | What position is publicly verified? |
| Mobilization | Is the actor likely to act? |
| Relationship | What is the organization's current or potential relationship? |

Distinguish **observed position** from **inferred interest**.

## D. Comparative lens

Use comparison to identify mechanisms, not to decorate the analysis.

Before comparing two jurisdictions, check:
- whether the institutions perform comparable functions;
- whether the policy instruments have similar legal force;
- whether implementation capacity differs;
- whether market structure or political incentives differ.

State the limit of the analogy.

## E. Public-value lens

Use when controversy involves more than compliance.

Potential questions:
- Which public interest is the policy trying to protect?
- Which rights or interests may conflict?
- What fairness concern drives the debate?
- Why might an otherwise lawful company action be publicly contested?
- What would make an external explanation credible to affected groups?
- Is the trust problem about outcomes, procedure, transparency, or representation?

This is where political-theory training can add value, but the analysis must return to concrete public-affairs choices.

## F. Issue life cycle

Classify the issue:

1. **Emergence** — fragmented concern, expert/media attention;
2. **Agenda setting** — official attention, hearings, speeches, consultations;
3. **Formalization** — draft rules, standards, legislation;
4. **Implementation** — operational guidance, institutional coordination;
5. **Enforcement** — cases, penalties, inspections, litigation;
6. **Normalization** — expectations stabilize and become routine.

The appropriate public-affairs response changes across stages.
