# Source Standard

Use this reference when researching current policy, regulation, enforcement, government action, or public controversy.

## 1. Evidence hierarchy

Prefer evidence in roughly this order:

1. **Primary legal or regulatory text**
   - laws, regulations, rules, official gazettes, formal consultation drafts;
2. **Official institutional material**
   - regulator notices, ministry documents, court decisions, enforcement announcements, official transcripts;
3. **Authoritative first-party material**
   - company filings/statements, industry-association documents, formal organizational releases;
4. **High-quality reporting**
   - reporting that names sources, dates events, and distinguishes fact from commentary;
5. **Academic / think-tank / professional research**
   - useful for context, comparison, and interpretation;
6. **Social media and informal commentary**
   - useful as evidence of discourse or reaction, not as a substitute for policy fact.

A lower-ranked source may be useful when it provides information unavailable elsewhere, but label the limitation.

## 2. Source-status discipline

Always distinguish among:

| Status | What it means for analysis |
|---|---|
| Effective rule | Current binding baseline, subject to scope and enforcement |
| Issued but not yet effective | Binding direction with implementation timing still relevant |
| Consultation / draft | Evidence of policy direction; provisions may change |
| Enforcement case | Evidence of interpretation or priority; avoid treating one case as universal |
| Official speech / signal | Useful for agenda and direction; weaker than formal instruments |
| Industry initiative | May shape norms without being state law |
| Media/social controversy | Can create reputational or agenda pressure without formal regulatory change |

## 3. Dating rules

For fast-moving issues:
- record the **event date** and **publication date** when they differ;
- prefer the newest primary source;
- do not call an older document “latest” without checking;
- identify when a later document supersedes or modifies an earlier one.

## 4. Attribution

Attach important claims to identifiable sources.

Use language that reflects evidence strength:
- “The regulation requires…”
- “The regulator stated…”
- “The draft proposes…”
- “Two enforcement actions suggest…”
- “Public reporting indicates…”
- “This analysis infers…”

Do not collapse these into the same level of certainty.

## 5. Missing evidence

When evidence is incomplete:
- say what is missing;
- state why it matters;
- identify the best next source or event to watch;
- avoid filling the gap with intuition.
